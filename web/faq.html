Good questions

Straight answers, including the uncomfortable ones.

The basics

What is Elvrek?
Elvrek is the control software for a mechanical CPR robot. It runs the piston that compresses the chest, times the breaths, charges the defibrillator and decides when the arm steps back for a shock. It runs on the QNX 8.0 real-time operating system.
Is it a medical device?
No. Elvrek is research software with no clearance or certification. It must never be connected to a person. Today the piston and the chest are a physics model. A future bench version will only ever be tested on a training manikin.
Who is it for?
Engineers, researchers, EMS trainers and device makers who want an open, testable controller for mechanical CPR, and anyone curious about how a safety critical machine can keep an AI component on a short leash.
Where does the name come from?
Elvrek is a coined name, short enough to shout across a room. It has no meaning in the languages we checked, which leaves it free to mean this.

Safety

Can the AI shock someone or stop compressions?
No. The rhythm classifier runs as an unprivileged process with its abilities locked. It can only write advice. The piston, the charger and the shock line are written only by the safety process, and QNX refuses the advisor any route to them. Stopping compressions needs a trusted signal such as a pulse input or a sharp rise in end-tidal CO2.
What happens if the software crashes?
If the advisor crashes or hangs, the supervisor restarts it and compressions continue on time. If the safety process itself stops, the hardware driver notices within 20 ms, lifts the piston to full recoil and holds it there. Nothing restarts the safety process automatically, because that decision belongs to a person.
How hard does it press?
It aims for 5.3 cm and never more than 6 cm. It stops pushing at 600 N of force and treats anything above 650 N as a fault. If a stiff chest cannot reach 5 cm under that ceiling, the run reports a miss rather than pressing harder.
Does it shock automatically?
The crew presses SHOCK, either when prompted or ahead of time while the charger fills. Either way the shock only happens if the arm is parked, the rhythm check agrees and the charger is full. If nobody presses within the 3 second window, compressions resume and the shock is offered again.

Results and honesty

How good is the rhythm check?
Better, but not good enough yet. We tuned it on one public database and then tested it once on another it had never seen. There it found ventricular fibrillation in 75% of windows and left 97.7% of normal rhythm alone, against AHA goals above 90% and 99%. We publish that because it is true. Until it meets the goals, the parked check should be read as a timed pause with a careful check, not a diagnosis.
Why do you report ticks instead of microseconds?
The current target runs in an emulator, and microsecond figures from an emulator mostly measure the emulator. Counting missed 1 ms ticks is honest on any platform. Hardware timing comes with the Raspberry Pi 5 build.
Do you compare yourselves with other products?
No. Other devices are measured under their own conditions, and comparing our model with their published numbers would not be fair. We report our own pass and fail results and invite you to run them.

Technology

Why QNX?
Because the hard part is not the maths, it is making sure the right code runs on time and the wrong code cannot reach the actuators. QNX gives fixed priority scheduling, timer pulses, message passing that carries priority, per process abilities, security policy types and a launch monitor. Elvrek uses all of them.
Can I run it without hardware?
Yes. The safety core builds and runs its 20 scenarios on any Linux machine. The full stack runs on QNX 8.0 in QEMU. No hardware is needed for either.
Is it open source?
Yes, under the Apache License 2.0. The ECG data used for evaluation comes from PhysioNet and is downloaded by a script rather than stored in the repository.
